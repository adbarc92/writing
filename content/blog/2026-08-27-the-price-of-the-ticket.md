The claim that one generation has been handed a worse deal than the one before it is made often enough, and loosely enough, that the readers best equipped to evaluate it have largely stopped listening. This is not an unreasonable response. The claim is usually advanced by setting a nominal price recalled from memory beside a nominal price from yesterday, a procedure that would establish the 1975 dollar as a marvel and the 1935 dollar as a miracle, and it is usually advanced by someone who wants something. I want to make the argument anyway, and I want to make it on the terms of the reader least disposed to accept it, because those terms are largely correct and the argument survives them.

Begin, then, with the strongest form of the rebuttal. Measured by what the money actually obtained, the household of 1975 was poorer than the household of today in nearly every respect a person experiences directly. Its car was substantially more likely to kill the people riding in it. Its medicine could not treat a long list of conditions that are now handled as a matter of routine, and its surgeries kept patients in hospital for weeks that are now measured in hours. Answering a factual question required a trip to a library and the better part of an afternoon. A household of modest means today holds goods — in computation, in communication, in diagnostic medicine, in the mundane safety of everyday objects — that the wealthiest household in the country in 1975 could not have obtained at any price, because they did not yet exist to be bought. The honest accounting of this is not a footnote to be cleared away before the real argument begins; it is the largest single fact about the period.

Moreover, the standard price indices, which are the instrument by which such comparisons are usually made, understate that improvement rather than overstating it. An index tracks the cost of a basket, and it handles a basket whose contents change in kind — a television that becomes a different sort of object, a telephone that becomes a general-purpose computer — imperfectly and, on the whole, conservatively. So if the question is whether the basket of things a person consumes has grown cheaper and better across fifty years, the answer is yes, and the answer is not close.

That, however, is one of two questions, and the two have been permitted to stand in for each other. The distinction I want turns on what a good is for. A consumption good is bought, used, and replaced; its price is disciplined by the cost of producing another one, and its supply expands when demand does. A threshold good is one that must be acquired before some other portion of a life becomes available at all — a place to live within reach of work, a credential without which certain work may not lawfully be performed, the care that makes it possible to work while raising a child. The distinguishing feature of a threshold good is not that it is expensive. It is that its price is set by what the other bidders can pay rather than by what it costs to make, because the quantity available is fixed by something other than the willingness of producers to produce.

The arithmetic follows that distinction closely enough to be worth setting down. Between 1975 and the present, median household income rose roughly sevenfold in nominal terms, which is to say it modestly outpaced general consumer inflation of something under sixfold. Over the same interval the median sale price of a home rose by a factor nearer eleven, and published tuition at a public four-year university by a factor nearer twenty. Converted into the only unit that matters to the person doing the paying: a median household in 1975 could purchase a median home with something on the order of three and a half years of its entire income, and a median household today requires something on the order of five. The figures are approximate, and the reader should treat them as orders of magnitude rather than measurements, but the ordering among them is not in serious dispute.

Additionally — and this cuts against my own presentation rather than for it, which is why it belongs here rather than in a footnote — the household comparison flatters the present. The household of 1975 more often contained one earner; the household of today more often contains two. A ratio computed on household income therefore conceals that the hours purchased by the second earner have already been spent buying back the ground the first one lost. Measured per hour worked rather than per household, the divergence is wider than the numbers above suggest.

What explains that divergence is not mysterious, and it is not, in the main, greed. The goods that grew cheap were the goods whose supply could expand: manufactured objects, which met containerized shipping and a global labor pool; computation, which met a fifty-year exponential; food, which met mechanization and chemistry. The goods that grew dear were the goods whose supply is set by permission. Housing is not scarce because lumber is scarce; it is scarce, in the places where the work is, because the quantity that may lawfully be built on a given parcel is fixed by the people who already live nearby. Credentials are not costly because instruction is costly; the marginal cost of instructing one additional student is small, and the binding constraints are accreditation, seat count, and the licensure that makes the credential a prerequisite rather than a preference. In each case a real and often defensible rule limits quantity, and in each case that limitation is capitalized into the price paid by whoever arrives next.

This suggests the complaint has been mis-stated by nearly everyone who makes it, including most of those who make it on my side of the argument. The complaint is not that life is worse, because it is not. It is that the relationship between effort and security has been altered — that the goods which convert income into a settled life have grown dear in something like proportion to how badly they are needed, while the goods that merely make life pleasant have grown cheap. A person may now afford, on an ordinary wage, a standard of daily consumption that would have astonished his grandparents, and may not be able to afford the house those grandparents bought on one income at twenty-six. Both facts are true. Only one of them is usually cited, and which one gets cited depends mostly on the conclusion the speaker arrived with.

If that account is correct it carries an obligation, because an argument that identifies a mechanism has thereby committed itself to a prediction and to a remedy. The prediction is that the divergence is sector-specific and supply-driven, and it would be embarrassed by any sustained period in which the constrained sectors — housing, credentials, care — fell in price relative to wages with no change in the rules that limit their quantity. The remedy that follows is correspondingly narrow. It is not a transfer, because a transfer directed at a good in fixed supply is bid into the price of that good and lands with whoever already owns it; subsidized borrowing for tuition and assistance toward down payments are the two clearest natural experiments available, and both behaved as the theory says they must. It is, instead, quantity: permission to build where the work is, credentials whose supply is allowed to answer demand for them, and care whose provision is not rationed by a licensure regime designed for a different century. The interesting question was never whether the arithmetic is unfair. It is which rules are setting the quantity, and who was in the room when they were written.
