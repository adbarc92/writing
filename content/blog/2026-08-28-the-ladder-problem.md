Consider a rule with three properties. It imposes a cost on anyone who wishes to do some particular thing; it is adopted by a body composed largely of people who have already done that thing; and it does not apply retroactively to them. Nothing in that description requires anyone to have acted in bad faith, and I want to be careful about this from the outset, because the pattern is usually described in a vocabulary of villainy that both misidentifies its cause and makes it harder to fix. The people who write such rules are ordinarily doing what the procedure asks of them, voting their interests and their sincere convictions in roughly the proportions the rest of us do. The defect is structural, and it lies in the composition of the body rather than the character of its members.

The individual instances have real justifications, and an argument that skips past them has not earned its conclusion. Zoning restrictions did not appear from nothing; they arose in part to prevent genuine nuisance, and the objection to a rendering plant beside a schoolyard is not a pretext. Occupational licensure screens incompetence in fields where the cost of an error is borne by someone who had no way to evaluate the practitioner in advance, and few readers would prefer an unlicensed anesthesiologist on the strength of a price comparison. Two-tier labor agreements, in which workers hired after a certain date receive a lower wage or a weaker pension than those hired before it, were in several well-documented cases the alternative to liquidation, and the jobs they preserved were real jobs held by real people. Each of these is a serious argument. None of them is the argument I am making.

The diagnostic question is not whether a rule is justified. It is whether the people who adopted it bear its cost. That question has an unusually clean empirical tell, which is the grandfather clause. When a legislature concludes that a standard is necessary to protect the public and in the same instrument exempts everyone currently practicing from meeting it, the legislature has recorded a judgment about its own belief in the standard. Either the practice is dangerous, in which case the exemption is indefensible, or it is not, in which case the requirement is. The clause is not evidence of corruption. It is evidence that the necessity was asserted against a party who was not present to contest it.

Zoning is the clearest case because its constituency problem is nearly geometric. The people empowered to decide how much housing may be built in a jurisdiction are the people who already live in that jurisdiction. The people who would benefit most from an additional unit do not live there — that is precisely what makes them beneficiaries — and so they are not constituents, cannot vote, and appear in the proceeding, if at all, as an abstraction invoked by whichever side finds them convenient. The result is not that homeowners conspire. It is that a body asked to weigh a diffuse benefit to absent strangers against a concentrated cost to present neighbors will, reliably and without anyone behaving badly, weigh it the way the room is composed.

Occupational licensing shows the same structure with a longer lag and a more respectable vocabulary. The number of hours of training required to obtain a license is set, in most jurisdictions, by boards drawn from the licensed practitioners of that occupation. It would be a considerable coincidence if the disinterested public-safety optimum happened so often to coincide with the level that restricts entry, and the hour requirements that have accumulated in cosmetology, interior design, and funeral direction are difficult to reconcile with the hazards those trades present. Where a genuine hazard does exist, the grandfather clause reappears on schedule, and with it the same admission: the standard was necessary enough to impose on the next cohort and not necessary enough to impose on the present one.

The two-tier contract completes the pattern by removing the last ambiguity about who is absent. A union local votes on terms that will apply in full to workers hired after the ratification date. Those workers are not outvoted. They are not employed, not identified, and in many cases not yet of working age; the class that bears the cost does not exist at the moment the cost is assigned to it. Whatever one concludes about the merits of any particular agreement, it is worth being clear that this is not a case of one faction defeating another. It is a case of a decision reached by a body from which the affected party is necessarily absent, and it is the cleanest illustration available that the problem is one of composition rather than of conflict.

The strongest objection to all of this deserves a hearing, because it is correct in a range of cases. Incumbents often do represent absent interests faithfully — parents vote on schools their children have not yet entered, and legislatures routinely bind future citizens — and the alternative to incumbent judgment is frequently no judgment at all, since experience is not distributed evenly and the people who know how a trade kills people are the people who have practiced it. I accept this. It narrows my claim rather than defeating it. The claim is not that incumbents should be excluded from decisions, which would be both impossible and foolish. It is that asymmetry of application is a specific and detectable defect, distinct from the ordinary difficulty of deciding on behalf of others, and that where a rule binds newcomers on grounds its authors declined to accept for themselves, the asymmetry is the thing to fix.

Five mechanisms follow, and I offer them in ascending order of how much institutional machinery they require.

The first is to sunset the grandfather clause rather than abolish it. Transition costs are real, and requiring every practitioner to re-credential overnight is not a serious proposal. Requiring that any exemption carry an expiry — five years, ten, a career — is. The effect is to force the question the clause was designed to avoid: if the standard is necessary, it binds everyone on a schedule, and if it is not, it binds no one. Either resolution is preferable to a permanent division of a trade into those the rule protects and those it excludes.

The second is to draw the boundary of the deciding body at least as wide as the effect of its decisions. A municipality deciding housing supply for a labor market it does not contain is choosing for people who are not its constituents, and the remedy is to locate that decision at the level — state, or metropolitan — at which those people are constituents. This is not centralization on principle, which has its own well-known failure modes and should be resisted where the effects genuinely are local. It is the narrower proposition that the jurisdiction should match the incidence.

The third is to price rather than prohibit wherever the underlying harm can be quantified at all. A prohibition on building creates scarcity and hands its entire value to those who already own the scarce thing; a charge levied on the same activity collects that value publicly and leaves the quantity to be determined by whether the activity is worth its cost. Impact fees, congestion charges, and taxation falling on land rather than on structures are the familiar instruments. The nuisance that zoning exists to prevent is a cost, and a cost is the kind of object that can be charged for.

The fourth is symmetric ratification. Where an agreement assigns different terms according to date of entry, it should either require ratification by a body weighted to include those it will bind — which for future hires means some form of trusteeship, imperfect but not unprecedented — or it should expire with the cohort that ratified it. A two-tier structure that dissolves within a defined period is a genuine emergency measure. One that persists indefinitely is a permanent transfer from people who were absent to people who were present, and the two deserve different names.

The fifth is a review triggered by the queue. Any rule whose operation produces a queue — a waiting list, a shortage, a licensure backlog, a vacancy rate below some stated floor — should be subject to automatic re-justification once that queue passes a threshold fixed in advance, and a person standing in the queue should have standing to compel the review. This is the mechanism I would keep if I could keep only one, because it addresses the root defect directly: a body accountable exclusively to those it has already admitted has no instrument for noticing the people it is keeping out, and a queue is the one signal those people generate merely by existing.

What unites the five is a single design commitment, which is that the legitimacy of a rule rests less on the quality of its justification than on the symmetry of its application. Justifications are cheap and usually sincere; the sincerity is not in question and never was. Symmetry is expensive, which is why it is the property worth building the procedure around — either by composing the deciding body so that the absent party is somehow present in it, or, where that cannot be arranged, by giving the rule an expiry short enough that the asymmetry does not have time to compound into an inheritance.
